Spray Foam vs Fiberglass: What the R-Value Numbers Actually Mean

October 24, 2024 8 Min Read

When evaluating insulation options for high-performance building envelopes, the discussion often begins and ends with R-value. However, relying solely on nominal R-value to dictate thermal performance can lead to significant misunderstandings of how a building will operate in real-world conditions.

Beyond Nominal R-Value: The Role of Air Infiltration

Nominal R-value measures a material's resistance to conductive heat flow in a static laboratory environment. While fiberglass batts may boast a competitive R-value per inch, this metric assumes zero air movement. In practical field applications, buildings are subjected to wind pressures, stack effect, and mechanical pressure differentials.

Fiberglass, being highly permeable, allows air to move through its matrix. When air infiltration occurs, the effective thermal resistance of the assembly drops precipitously. Closed-cell spray polyurethane foam (ccSPF), conversely, serves as both a thermal insulator and a robust air barrier. By arresting convective loops within stud cavities and sealing penetration points, ccSPF maintains its rated thermal performance under dynamic real-world conditions.

Thermal Bridging and Continuous Insulation

Traditional cavity insulation, regardless of the material, suffers from thermal bridging—the accelerated transfer of heat through highly conductive structural members like wood or steel studs. While installing high R-value batts between studs is common practice, the framing itself can account for 15% to 25% of the wall area, creating significant pathways for energy loss.

Spray foam insulation, particularly when applied as exterior continuous insulation (ci), effectively decouples the structural framing from the exterior environment, mitigating thermal bridging. Even when used solely in cavities, spray foam's ability to expand and fill irregular voids ensures a consistent thermal blanket, unlike batts which are prone to compression and poor fitment around electrical boxes and plumbing runs.

Moisture Physics Considerations

Understanding thermal performance requires understanding moisture movement. Warm air holds more moisture than cold air; when warm, humid air infiltrates a wall cavity and contacts a cold surface, condensation occurs. Because fiberglass allows air movement, it introduces the risk of interstitial condensation in colder climates if vapor retarders are improperly detailed.

Closed-cell spray foam acts as a Class II vapor retarder (at typical installation thicknesses), resisting bulk moisture and limiting vapor diffusion. This inherent property simplifies the assembly physics, reducing the risk of moisture accumulation and subsequent structural degradation or mold proliferation.
