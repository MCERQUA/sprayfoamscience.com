# FAQ Research — real PAA + niche questions, source: DataForSEO PAA pulls 2026-08-20

## Chemistry & Fundamentals
1. What's the actual chemical difference between open-cell and closed-cell spray foam? — Both are two-component polyurethane systems (an "A side" isocyanate and a "B side" polyol resin) that react on mixing. Closed-cell foam is formulated with a blowing agent that creates small, closed gas-filled cells, giving it higher density, higher R-value per inch, and structural rigidity. Open-cell foam uses water as the blowing agent, creating larger, open cells — lower density, lower R-value per inch, but more vapor-permeable and typically lower cost.
2. Which has a higher R-value, open-cell or closed-cell? — Closed-cell typically runs about R-6 to R-7 per inch; open-cell typically runs about R-3.5 to R-3.7 per inch, because the closed gas-filled cell structure resists heat transfer more effectively than the open, air-permeable structure.

## R-Value & Performance
3. Does spray foam's real-world R-value match the lab-tested number? — Lab R-values are measured under controlled, steady-state lab conditions. Real-world "effective" R-value is also shaped by installation quality, air-sealing (spray foam's real advantage is often air-sealing, not just R-value), and thermal bridging through framing that isn't foam-covered.
4. Is a higher R-value always better? — Not necessarily past the point of diminishing returns for a given climate zone's code requirement — air-sealing quality and moisture management often matter more to real comfort and energy performance than chasing R-value alone.

## The Reputation Question (the differentiated cluster)
5. Why is spray foam insulation now considered a "red flag" by some buyers and agents? — Almost always traceable to installation problems, not the chemistry itself: off-ratio mixing causing shrinkage/pulling away from framing, application over damp substrate, or improper thickness for the assembly — all of which are root-cause application failures, not a defect inherent to the material.
6. Why do some insurance companies flag spray foam insulation? — Underwriters are responding to real claims history tied to bad installs — moisture trapped behind foam that was applied over a wet or improperly prepped substrate, or foam blocking necessary roof ventilation in an assembly that needed it. A properly specified, correctly installed system doesn't carry these risks the way a bad install does.
7. Can mold grow behind spray foam insulation? — Yes, if moisture gets trapped between the foam and the substrate — usually from applying foam to a damp surface, or from a moisture-barrier design mismatch for the specific assembly (roof deck vs. wall cavity vs. crawlspace each need different approaches).
8. Why is it harder to sell a house with spray foam insulation? — Buyer and lender caution has grown due to real cases of bad installs, but a documented, code-compliant, professionally installed system is generally not the liability the broader narrative suggests — the issue is usually verifying installation quality, not the material's presence.
9. Is it safe to be in a house after spray foam installation? — Reputable manufacturers specify a re-entry/re-occupancy time (commonly in the 24-hour range, varies by product) to allow off-gassing of VOCs from the curing reaction to drop to safe levels. Following the manufacturer's specified re-entry time is the controlling factor, not a fixed universal number.

## Off-Ratio & Curing
10. What actually causes spray foam to shrink or pull away from framing? — Most commonly, off-ratio mixing (the two components not delivered in the correct 1:1 ratio by the proportioning equipment) or application at the wrong ambient/substrate temperature, both of which prevent a complete, stable chemical reaction.
11. How long does spray foam take to cure? — Tack-free time is typically minutes; full cure to final properties is typically 24 hours, though this varies by product and ambient conditions.
12. What does off-ratio spray foam smell like, and does the smell mean something is wrong? — A persistent, strong chemical odor well past the manufacturer's stated cure/off-gas window can indicate incomplete reaction (off-ratio mixing), which is a genuine installation-quality signal worth having inspected.

## Moisture & Vapor Physics
13. Can spray foam trap moisture in a wall? — Closed-cell foam is also a vapor retarder; used in the wrong assembly (e.g., blocking necessary drying in a climate/assembly that needs to dry to one side), it can trap moisture that would otherwise escape — which is an assembly-design issue, not a universal flaw in the material.
14. Does spray foam need a separate vapor barrier? — Depends on the assembly and climate zone; closed-cell foam above a certain thickness often satisfies vapor-retarder requirements itself, while open-cell foam is vapor-permeable and may still need a separate strategy depending on the assembly.

## DIY vs. Professional
15. Can DIY spray foam kits achieve the same quality as professional application? — DIY kits use pre-measured, pressurized cylinders rather than a calibrated proportioning machine, so they're more exposed to ratio drift from temperature and pressure variation — the same off-ratio failure mode professional equipment is specifically built to control for.
16. Why does spray foam need special equipment instead of just a spray gun? — Professional rigs use a heated hose and proportioner to hold the two components at a precise, consistent 1:1 ratio and temperature under varying field conditions — control that a fixed-cylinder DIY kit can't replicate across a whole job.

## Fire & Code
17. Does spray foam need to be covered by a thermal or ignition barrier? — Most codes require an approved thermal barrier (like drywall) or ignition barrier over spray foam in occupied space, based on the foam's fire-test rating for that specific assembly.
18. Is spray foam a fire hazard by itself? — Like most organic building materials, uncovered foam has fire performance characteristics that codes account for through required barrier coverings — code compliance for the full assembly, not the foam alone, is the controlling factor.

## Cost & Comparison
19. Why does spray foam cost more than fiberglass? — Material cost, specialized equipment, and licensed-applicator labor all factor in; the comparison is usually most meaningful when air-sealing performance (spray foam's core advantage) is weighed alongside R-value, not R-value alone.
20. What actually drives the price per board foot? — Foam type (open- vs closed-cell), job accessibility, substrate prep required, and regional labor/equipment costs.
